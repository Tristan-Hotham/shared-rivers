Who shares the world's rivers?

{{basins.n|int}} river basins cross a national border. Together they drain {{basins.area_pct|pc}} of the world's land outside Antarctica and are home to {{basins.pop_pct|pc}} of its people. One map of the rivers, the basins and the countries that share them, with an honest look at why nobody quite agrees how long the longest river is.

Rivers do not stop at borders. Rain that falls in the Ethiopian highlands reaches the Mediterranean through Sudan and Egypt; water from the Black Forest flows past Vienna, Bratislava, Budapest and Belgrade on its way to the Black Sea. A river basin is all the land that drains to one river, and when that land lies in more than one country, everything upstream matters to everyone downstream.

This study puts the world's shared basins on one map, using the Transboundary Freshwater Dispute Database (TFDD) at Oregon State University, which keeps the register of international river basins. It adds the rivers themselves from Natural Earth, measured end to end, and the share of each country's water that flows in from its neighbours from the UN Food and Agriculture Organization's AQUASTAT.     03 · The longest riversMeasured on one map, and why the answer moves

    Which river is longest sounds like a question with an answer. It is not, quite. A river's length is usually taken from its mouth to its most distant source, and every part of that is a choice. Which headwater counts as the source? Where does a river end when it reaches the sea through an estuary or a maze of delta channels? And how finely do you measure a line that wiggles at every scale, from great bends down to single meanders?

    To make the comparison fair I measured every river system the same way, on one map: the Natural Earth 1:10 million river lines. I joined the {{rivers.n_lines|int}} lines into a network, found each system's mouth, and followed it back to the most distant source. On that map the Nile is the longest system at {{rivers.top.0.len|int}} km, then the {{rivers.top.1.name|raw}} ({{rivers.top.1.len|int}} km), the {{rivers.top.2.name|raw}} ({{rivers.top.2.len|int}} km) and the {{rivers.top.3.name|raw}} ({{rivers.top.3.len|int}} km).

    Every one of these is shorter than the figures in an atlas, and that is the point. A map at 1:10 million smooths out the bends, so a river traced on it is shorter than the same river traced on a detailed survey. Measure the same paths on Natural Earth's coarser 1:50 million lines and they shrink again: the Nile to {{rivers.top.0.len50|int}} km, the Yangtze to {{rivers.yangtze.len50|int}} km. Simplify the lines further and the shrinking continues; there is no scale at which it settles down.

    The coarser the map, the shorter the river

    The map also has to decide where a river begins and ends. Natural Earth's Amazon follows the Ucayali all the way into the Andes of southern Peru, to {{rivers.amazon.src_lat_abs|f1}}°S, close to where most accounts put its source. What shortens it most is the other end: the map's coastline opens into the Amazon's vast estuary at {{rivers.amazon.mouth_lon_abs|f1}}°W, so the river meets the sea well before the open ocean. Its Nile follows the White Nile and Lake Victoria up the Kagera to a source in {{rivers.nile.src_country|raw}}. Choose the Blue Nile instead and the Nile system is {{rivers.nile_blue.len|int}} km on the same map. Choose the Mississippi's own source rather than the Missouri's, and the Mississippi is {{rivers.miss_own.len|int}} km rather than {{rivers.miss.len|int}}.

    Published lengths have the same problems, solved in different ways. Encyclopaedia Britannica gives the Nile as about 6,650 km and the Amazon as 6,400 km, but measurements that take the Amazon's mouth through the Pará estuary and its source in the far Peruvian Andes have made it the longer of the two. There is no referee: no body decides river lengths, and the two rivers are close enough that the choices, not the rivers, decide the ranking.

    05 · Upstream, downstreamEgypt's water starts in other countries

    Sharing a basin matters most to the countries at the bottom of it. AQUASTAT's dependency ratio is the share of a country's renewable water that comes from outside its borders, in rivers that flow in and groundwater that seeps across. For {{countries.dep_n_over50|int}} of the {{countries.dep_n|int}} countries with a value, more than half of their water starts somewhere else.

    06 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Basins. The TFDD register lists {{basins.n|int}} international basins in its 2024 version, with the countries that share each and their area and population. The polygons on the map are the TFDD's 2018 spatial data for {{basins.n_poly|int}} of them (the {{basins.n_nopoly|int}} basins added since, the Loa, the Allahein and the Ems, are counted but not drawn), split into {{basins.n_bcu|int}} basin country units, which give each country's share. I count sovereign countries: the TFDD also lists disputed areas such as Abyei or Aksai Chin as riparians, and I leave those out of the count but show them in the panels. The West Bank is counted as Palestine and French Guiana as France. Population is the TFDD's estimate for 2022; the world total is the World Bank's.

    Land. The share of land is the TFDD basin area over the land area of the Natural Earth 1:10m outlines outside Antarctica ({{basins.land_mkm2|f1}} million km²). The share of each country in shared basins compares its basin country units with its Natural Earth area, so small differences in outline can push it slightly over or under; I cap it at 100%.

    Rivers. The river lines are Natural Earth's 1:10 million river and lake centrelines (the edition with stroke weights), {{rivers.n_lines|int}} lines in all. I split and joined them into a network, left out canals and two channels that link separate rivers (the Casiquiare between the Orinoco and the Amazon, and the Kovzha along the Volga to Baltic waterway), and found mouths at the sea or at five terminal lakes such as the Aral Sea and Lake Chad. Each system's length is the network distance from its mouth to its most distant source. Countries along a river are found by sampling its line every kilometre. These are map lengths, consistently measured, not survey lengths.

    Water from abroad. The AQUASTAT dependency ratio for {{countries.dep_n|int}} countries, as downloaded from AQUASTAT in 2017 and published in a public teaching repository; the values are for 2013 to 2017. AQUASTAT revises its estimates rarely and slowly, but newer values may differ.

    What this is not. Sharing a basin is not the same as sharing water fairly, or fighting over it. The TFDD also records treaties, river basin organisations and disputes, which this study does not map. Basins hide groundwater aquifers, which have borders of their own, and the population and area figures are estimates. The river lengths are those of one generalised map, and the published lengths are quoted only to show how they differ.
